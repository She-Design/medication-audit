The product

What we're building

Households keep a drawer full of medicine that only one person understands. Boxes lose their leaflets, nobody remembers what the half-used one was for, and the person who does know isn't always the one standing at the cabinet.

The biggest pain isn't not knowing what's in the drawer — it's standing there at 2am having to decide alone, so the app's job is to make the household's own knowledge survive without the person who holds it, and make the pharmacist one tap away when it isn't enough.

The key task

Find what we have, know what it's for in our own words, and whether that's still true. Everything is measured against that sentence. Entry has to be cheap or the knowledge never gets in; entries have to visibly age or the knowledge quietly stops being knowledge.

Who it's for

Couples and small households sharing a cabinet, plus people living alone. Defined by three traits: low pharmaceutical literacy — they know "the white box for headaches", not "ibuprofen 400 mg"; not near a pharmacy — restocking is a planned trip; shared responsibility, uneven knowledge — one person buys and remembers, everyone else needs to act without them.

The line we don't cross

The app never tells anyone what to take. It reports what the household owns, what the household wrote about it, and mechanical facts — dates, counts, matching ingredient names. No diagnosis, no recommendation, no dose. That boundary keeps the product honest and out of medical-device territory, and it shapes every decision that follows.

Read this before the findings

No app was installed, no medicine was added, nothing was timed. All competitor evidence is vendor marketing, store listings, store metadata and other people's reviews.

No user research has been done at all — not one interview, survey or observation with a household. Every statement about our users is a hypothesis inherited from the brief, not a finding. Section 4 treats them as such, and marks the ones with no evidence behind them.

Three patterns across the market

1

A register is table stakes for credibility, but it is not what wins users

An earlier draft of this research claimed the opposite. The store data breaks it in both directions: BEEP has no pharmaceutical data at all and 500K+ installs; mojApteczka has the Polish national register and 100+ installs with no rating on Android. A register buys autofill and credibility — not distribution or retention.

2

Almost nobody in this category is paid by the person using the product

Medisafe and MyTherapy are paid by pharma. Bring! by retailers. The Cyprus pharmacy apps by an OTC brand banner sitting directly above the on-duty list. Red Cross by donors, the NHS by taxpayers, Apteczka Domowa by a corporation's goodwill. The only products charging users directly are solo-developer apps at £25–30 a year — and none has an audience.

3

Expiry is always typed by hand — the one field the category exists for

Barcodes do not carry expiry dates. BEEP says so outright: scan the barcode, then enter the date. Every product here, registry-backed or not, asks a human for it — and it is the single largest source of anger in the category. Roughly half of BEEP's captured one-star reviews are people who expected a scanner to read it: “to much typing… not 2mins per item” (1★, 22 Dec 2025).

The lesson is not “don't ask for the date”. It is never imply you won't.

Three differences — where nobody is standing

1

Everyone designs for a patient or a caregiver. Nobody designs for a household that doesn't know what it owns

Home Med Cabinet's own store screenshots use Lisinopril, Metformin and Atorvastatin as sample data, under the headline “Grandma has six. Dad has three. You have one app.” That is chronic prescription management for an ageing relative. Every competitor assumes the user already knows what their medicines are called.

2

Everyone answers “what is this medicine?” from an authority. Nobody answers “what did we decide this was for?”

mojApteczka has Notes as one card among twelve, at the end of its feature grid. Everywhere else the household's own words are a footnote to the official leaflet. For us they are the primary description and the authority is optional — currently the clearest thing we own.

3

Storage location exists everywhere as a filing system. Nobody connects it to safety

Home Med Cabinet models Kitchen, Car and Travel Kit; mojApteczka groups by location. Both use it to help you find things. No competitor found links location to temperature — and a medicine in a car in Cyprus in August is a different object from one in a kitchen drawer.

Not proven

That heat is a real problem in Cypriot homes. The brief asserts it; no measurement, guidance document or user report has been gathered. See hypothesis H6.

A fourth, worth as much as the three above

Nobody ships household sharing with a clean data declaration. The apps that share — mojApteczka, Medisafe, MyTherapy — all declare data shared with third parties, because ads or pharma fund them. The ones with clean declarations are local-only, and their reviews are full of people who lost everything when a phone died. Shared and clean is an empty position.

Benchmark

The parameter benchmarked is inherited trust — can a second person rely on what the first one recorded, without re-checking, and know when to stop relying on it? Five etalons, none of them medical, scored 1–5 on eight criteria.

Nobody clears 31 of 40, and no product is strong on more than five of eight criteria: there is no etalon to copy, only one to assemble. The sharpest lesson is the lowest score on the board — Apple Medical ID is the best product here at surviving its author and the worst at knowing it has gone stale. One written in 2019 presents itself with exactly today's authority. That is the failure our product is one design decision away from.

Three mechanics for the MVP

M1

One-tap freshness confirmation — from Waze

A Waze report carries its age, passing drivers confirm or dismiss it with one tap, and dismissals shorten its life. Applied: whenever a medicine surfaces — opened, searched, or in the monthly digest — one tap answers still in the drawer? Each tap restamps the entry; an entry nobody has confirmed in a year says so on its face instead of pretending.

This targets the failure other companies' users describe directly: “I added new packs, but the summary still shows the medicines I added before, which I no longer use.” (1★, 23 Dec 2025, translated.)

M2

Two severity registers, plus proof of life — from Nest Protect

Heads-Up is a spoken early warning; the alarm is a different register entirely for critical levels. It runs a Self Test every 200 seconds, a monthly Sound Check of its own speaker and horn, and glows green at lights-out so silence is evidence rather than absence. Applied: a four-level severity scale, plus a monthly digest that says checked, nothing needs you even when there is nothing to report.

M3

Access by someone who is not the owner — from Apple Medical ID

Allergies, conditions and emergency contacts are readable from the Lock Screen without a passcode, by a paramedic who has never met you. Applied: an emergency view of the cabinet reachable without the household account. This is the job statement rendered as a mechanism, and no competitor found ships it.

Take the access model and explicitly not the staleness model — Medical ID scores 1 on visible decay.

One that will not work — crowd corroboration

Waze and Wikipedia are trustworthy because many independent strangers examine the same claim. The mechanism requires a crowd. A household is two to four people: nobody outvotes a wrong entry, a reputation score carries no signal, and the person who typed an entry is the same one who would confirm it. One reviewer states the problem for us — “a medicine database held by only one person in the home makes no sense” (1★, 22 Nov 2025).

Take the gesture, refuse the epistemology. Freshness must come from the object — the printed date, elapsed time, the dose log — never from consensus.

Section 3

Patterns

Eight interaction patterns considered for the key task, separated by where retrieval starts — so that they are genuinely different mechanics rather than variants of one.

PatternRetrieval starts fromVerdict
LedgerA word in the record Fails the user we designed for: demands vocabulary they don't have, and an empty result is indistinguishable from “we don't own any”
Need-firstThe symptomSelected — see below
Spatial mirrorA place Strong second. Highest maintenance cost: the map silently lies when someone moves a box
Object-firstThe physical pack Blocked in our market — the Cyprus register has no barcode field, so there is nothing to resolve a scan against
Label-firstThe object, off-screen Needs a ritual most households won't perform; the sticker outlives the record
EpisodicA memory of an event Needs months of history; episodic memory is the first thing that fails at 2am
Static sheetNothing — you just read it Right for emergency access (M3), breaks past ~10 items
ConversationalA sentenceRejected outright — see below
Selected — need-first

The index is not medicines but reasons — throat, fever, allergy, cuts, stomach. Tap one, see what the household owns tagged to it, in the household's own words.

1. The audience is defined by low pharmaceutical literacy. They know “the white box for headaches”, not “cetirizine”. A search box demands vocabulary the brief says they lack; a list of reasons demands none.

2. The task is a question, not a lookup. The user arrives with a symptom, not a product name. Need-first is the only pattern whose front door matches how the problem presents.

3. It makes the household's own words pay for themselves. The notes and tags become the index, so writing a note has an immediate visible payoff. Every other pattern treats notes as decoration — and staleness is the documented killer in this category.

What can go wrong: it can read as advice. Ordering implies ranking; an empty category implies “you can't treat this”. Both must be handled in wording, and the wording is the safety surface.

Rejected outright — conversational

Not “not yet”, on three independent grounds. It crosses the line we never cross — a sentence answering “what do we have for a sore throat” is a recommendation however it is hedged. It breaks offline, and the one moment we exist for is the one with no signal. And it cannot be calibrated — a fluent sentence is maximally confident by construction, while our whole trust model is never claiming more than we know.

Home Med Cabinet already ships AI-generated side-effect and interaction text, written by a solo developer with no stated medical review. That is the road, and it is visible.

Section 4

Conclusions

Each gap becomes a falsifiable hypothesis, with the section it depends on, the evidence for and against, and how to test it. Where a hypothesis has no evidence at all, it says so.

H1Entry friction is the thing that kills this product
Hypothesis
If adding one medicine takes longer than ~30 seconds, the cabinet goes stale within three months and the app becomes worse than useless, because it lies.
Relies on
§1 Competitors — market pattern 3 and the review evidence
For
BEEP's 1.9 rating over 1,910 ratings, driven by typing complaints; the Apteczka Domowa staleness reviews
Not proven
The 30-second threshold. Asserted in the brief, never measured anywhere.
Test
Install five apps, add one real medicine to each, record taps and seconds. Needs a phone.
H2A bundled register removes most of the entry cost without any scanning
Hypothesis
Brand-name autocomplete from the Cyprus register — which returns the active ingredient for free — cuts entry to a name, a date and a place.
Relies on
§1 Competitors — market pattern 1
For
The register exists, is open, CC BY 4.0, 6,369 products with brand name, active ingredient, strength, form and ATC code
Against
It has no prescription/OTC field, so it cannot tell us what a household can actually buy — which the kit checklist needs
Not proven
That autocomplete measurably reduces entry time for this audience.
H3One-tap confirmation keeps the cabinet true
Hypothesis
A single confirm/gone gesture, offered where the user already is, keeps entries fresh enough that the cabinet stays trustworthy without deliberate maintenance.
Relies on
§2 Benchmark — M1
For
Waze's report lifecycle works at scale on exactly this mechanic
Not proven
That it transfers to a household of three. Waze's version is backed by crowd corroboration, which we have explicitly rejected — the gesture is being lifted out of the system that makes it work.
Test
The first thing to put in front of real users.
H4Need-first beats a ledger for this audience
Hypothesis
Users with low pharmaceutical literacy find the right medicine faster from a list of reasons than from a search box.
Relies on
§3 Patterns
For
Nothing from users. The argument is derived from the brief's description of the audience, which is itself unvalidated.
Not proven
The entire premise. This is the weakest-supported major decision in the project.
Test
A five-person unmoderated test with both layouts and a real cabinet.
H5There is a household audience distinct from the caregiver audience
Hypothesis
Households managing OTC medicines are a real, reachable market — not just the caregiver market with less money.
Relies on
§1 Competitors — difference 1
For
No competitor serves them
Against
Every shipping competitor has drifted to the caregiver, and drift that consistent usually reflects where the demand is
Not proven
That the household audience will pay for anything, or open the app more than three times a year.
Test
Interviews. None have been done.
H6Heat makes storage location a safety signal in Cyprus
Hypothesis
Cypriot indoor summer temperatures routinely exceed the “store below 25 °C” label, making where a medicine is kept a genuine warning rather than a filing detail.
Relies on
§1 Competitors — difference 3, where the finding is only that nobody else does this
Not proven
This is an assertion in the brief with no evidence gathered — no temperature data, no official storage guidance, no user report. It is the most confident unsupported claim in the project, and it is load-bearing: one of only two things that make this product specifically Cypriot.
Test
Indoor summer temperature data for Cypriot homes, plus what the Pharmaceutical Services say about storage.
H7This product can be funded without pharma money or OTC advertising
Hypothesis
A product whose warnings must be neutral can be funded some other way than the two models this market actually uses.
Relies on
§1 Competitors — market pattern 2
Against
Almost nobody in the category is paid by the user; the only ones who try are solo developers with no audience
Not proven
Any viable alternative. None has been identified.
Test
A decision, not an experiment — and one that shapes the account model, so it is needed early.
H8No-account emergency access is the differentiator
Hypothesis
A cabinet view readable by someone who is not the owner — offline, without an account — is the clearest expression of the job statement and a position nobody occupies.
Relies on
§2 Benchmark (M3) and §1 Competitors (the fourth difference)
For
No competitor found ships it; Apple Medical ID proves the interaction is viable
Against
The most expensive thing on the list, and it holds special-category health data outside the account boundary — a GDPR problem as much as a UX one
Not proven
That users want it, or would trust it.

What to do next, in order

1

Settle H6 and H7 — they can invalidate the positioning

Both are answerable without users: one from temperature and storage-guidance data, one from a decision about money.

2

H1 needs a phone

Install the five benchmark apps, add a real medicine to each, time it. Nothing in a browser substitutes for this.

3

H4 and H5 need people

Nothing in this document replaces talking to five households. Until that happens, the audience section of the brief is a well-argued guess.
