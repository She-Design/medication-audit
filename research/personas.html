Section 1

Personas

Scaffolds, not people: role-titles rather than invented names, no ages, no biographies. Every one of those details would be [?], and a deck of confident invented detail is precisely the failure this project guards against. Every quote below is real and dated — and, with one exception, none was said by the persona.

P1

The Keeper

Primary

The one who buys and remembers. Does the first inventory, and is the reason there is anything in the cabinet to read.

Context

  • A “One person buys and remembers.” Corroborated from outside by exactly one stranger.
  • B The behaviour is real at scale — BEEP has 500K+ installs and its reviewers catalogue emergency food kits and 500-item stock. Whether it is real for medicines, in a home: [?]
  • B The drawer at first inventory is boxless, half-used strips — “when you build the database for the first time many medicines have no box.” One reviewer, generalised.
  • R Brand-literate, ingredient-blind. They have a word for what they bought, and it is the brand. Of 375 Cypriots asked at pharmacies, 87.2% knew Panadol contains paracetamol; Depon 50.8%, Solpadeine 49.7%, Buscopan-plus 28.5%, Apotel 14.1%; only 28.2% knew the maximum daily dose. Same shape in Sweden, Belgium and the US.
  • R Heat. The label says below 25 °C. Nicosia's July–August daily mean is 30.0 °C; un-cooled Cypriot rooms measured at 29.3 °C mean; “almost 30%” of the population cannot stay cool at home. A car: 60 °C inside a medication bag in the nearest measured analogue. Nobody official in Cyprus tells them any of this. [?] where a Cypriot household keeps the boxes; [?] the temperature inside that cupboard.
  • C One real Keeper — the author is building this for their own household. [?] whether that household is in Cyprus; its size; its languages.

Jobs

  • B Get the drawer into the app once, and never do it again.
  • A Keep it true without deliberately maintaining it.
  • A Make their knowledge survive without them — held on behalf of P2.
  • A Know before running out. [?] for Cyprus.

Pain points

  • B The first inventory is hours long, and everything that happens to it afterwards decides the relationship.
  • B Required fields — EAN, photo, a calendar permission to add one medicine. Seven years of one-star reviews.
  • B Typing the expiry date — the one field the category exists for, and no scanner fills it. Roughly half of BEEP's 183 captured one-star reviews.
  • B The list going stale underneath them — the existential failure.
  • B Losing it on a new phone: “when the phone dies you start over typing how many throat tablets you have.”
  • B The obligation itself: “it's a hassle track everything down and go back to look at it.”

Trust triggers

Builds trust
  • Trying it before surrendering an identity — the most-endorsed negative review in the dataset (247) is an account wall.
  • Every structured field optional.
  • Archive rather than delete; duplicate check at entry — asked for by name.
  • Silence — “This is not an app I want to interact with daily.”
  • A data declaration that matches the marketing. Nobody in the category ships household sharing with a clean one.
Causes hesitation
  • A cap arriving after the cataloguing — “don't blackmail users into paying after they have spent hours.”
  • Any implication that a scanner will read the date.
  • Advertising near medicine — the Cyprus precedent is an OTC banner above the duty-pharmacy list.
  • Notifications that nag, or that fail — either makes the whole app untrustworthy.
  • Sync that diverges.

Quote

“I spent hours and hours logging inventory and trying to get everything organized only to find out that it is not unlimited on the number of items you can store.” Sortly, 1★, 3 Mar 2019 · 23 found helpful · a business inventory, not a medicine cabinet
“I want to build this up for myself and for my family… I would like to be the maintenance first user… of course, not to take it as a main source.” The author, 14 Sep 2026 — the only words here from inside the intended audience; the second half governs how the first may be used
The one real Keeper — what it buys, and the liability

Buys: the slow failure becomes observable — staleness only appears after months, and n = 1 longitudinal beats n = 5 snapshot for that one failure. Entry friction can finally be timed. One thermometer, one summer, closes the last link in the heat question.

Liability: this Keeper will not feel most of P1's pain points — every drop-off in the review corpus happens to someone who is not motivated. They cannot experience their own onboarding. And whoever has read the Cyprus register now knows what is inside Depon — so ingredient-blindness cannot be tested on them.

P2

The Other Person

Secondary

The household member standing at the cabinet without the Keeper. The person the product exists for — and the one we have never heard from.

Context

  • A Did not buy it, was not there when it was bought, cannot read the foil strip. May never have installed the app — the brief plans for exactly this.
  • B The structural blind spot: we hold no quote from this person in any source. Reviewers are people who installed something; the absence is observed, and its inevitability is an inference. [?] whether this is why the whole category designs for someone else.

Jobs

  • A Answer three questions in under a minute, without help and without signal.
  • A Act without waking the Keeper.
  • A Know when to stop trusting what they are reading — Apple Medical ID scores 1 of 5 on visible decay.
  • A Get out to a pharmacist when the household's knowledge runs out. Undecided in v1.

Trust triggers

Builds trust

Causes hesitation

Quote

[?] No quote from this population exists in any source we hold. Nobody reviews an app by mentioning that they live alone. Marked, rather than dressed up.

Why P1 is primary

1

Every drop-off we have evidence for is a Keeper drop-off. Account wall, required fields, typing the date, the cap, phone change, sync divergence, staleness, notifications — all happen to the person doing the work.

2

If P1 quits, P2 and P3 have nothing to open. The Keeper is a dependency, not a peer.

3

It is the only persona with real evidence behind its behaviour — and the only one with a live instance.

The tension, stated rather than smoothed

P1 is primary, but the job statement does not belong to them. The brief measures this product by the person standing at the cabinet without the Keeper — that is P2. So: P1 governs the friction budget; P2 governs the measure of success. Optimising only for P1 produces another competitor — the drift every shipping product in the category shows.

Section 2

Jobs to be done

Two rules. Every situation is a real moment — something you could film, not a state of mind. And a job reaches the main list only if a real person's words support it; everything resting on the brief's argument alone is in Hypotheses, including some of the most load-bearing beliefs in the project. No job names anything we would build.

The main job

When someone at home needs medicine and the person who knows isn't there, I want to find out what we have and whether it's still good, so that I don't have to guess.

P2, in the moment · held on their behalf by P1   B one sentence, one stranger: “a medicine database held by only one person in the home makes no sense.” That supports the premise. It does not support the scene the brief wraps around it — 2am, a fever, foil strips by phone light — which is in Hypotheses.

The four jobs that lead to it

J1

When I tip the whole drawer out onto the table, I want to get through all of it in one go, so that I never have to start from nothing again.

P1 B the strongest pattern in the corpus — “I spent hours and hours logging inventory…” (Sortly, 1★, 23 helpful)

J2

When I get home with a new box, or take the last tablet from a strip, I want to say what changed in seconds, so that it never becomes a job I put off.

P1 B “I don't want a hassle, I just want to quickly open an app and jot down an item before I forget.” (Bring!, 1★, 21 helpful) · A the brief prices this at 30 seconds — never measured anywhere

J3

When I look to see whether we've got something, I want to know the answer is still true, so that I don't open the drawer to check anyway.

P1 and P2 B the existential failure — “the summary still shows the medicines I added before, which I no longer use. It makes a mess.” (Apteczka Domowa, 1★, 23 Dec 2025)

J4

When I change my phone, I want everything I already worked out to still be there, so that one evening's work doesn't have to happen again.

P1 B “when the phone dies you start over typing how many throat tablets you have and until when they're valid.” (Apteczka Domowa, 1★, 30 Nov 2025)

Emotional jobs

E1

When my phone buzzes at me about medicine, I want it to be because something actually needs me, so that I don't get into the habit of ignoring it.

P1 B “I'm not playing Duolingo, I have clinical depression.” (MyTherapy, 2★) · “This is not an app I want to interact with daily.” (Bring!, 2★)

E2

When I've already given up an evening to this, I want the terms not to change on me afterwards, so that I don't feel stupid for having bothered.

P1 B the most resented pattern in the dataset — “don't blackmail users into paying after they have spent hours putting data into your app.” (BEEP, 1★)

Social job

S1

When I'm away and someone at home texts me asking which one to take, I want them to work it out without me, so that nothing waits on my answering.

P1 B “What I miss is automatic exchange of data between the apps on different phones.” (Apteczka Domowa, 4★) · “one of the main reasons I used this was how it syncs with my partners accounts.” (Medisafe, 1★). Only one social job — every other belongs to P2 or P3, and we hold no words from either.

What this matrix is honest about

Of 51 importance cells: 7 rest on real people's words — the P1 column of the evidenced jobs, minus the Main row. 2 are the brief's assertions, 6 are structurally not that persona's job, and 36 are [?]. Seven-tenths of the matrix is a blank we are choosing not to fill.

Two lines carry the most weight. H2: the product's entire navigation is built on a job no person has ever expressed to us. And J3: the one job that is both existential and unopposed is served by a mechanism the locked scope does not yet contain.

Section 4

Conclusions

Three jobs for the MVP core

Criterion: importance 3 to the primary persona, and the market does not address it in a form that works. The three are the product's spine: put it in cheaply, keep it true, make it reachable by the others.

1

J2 — keeping up while my hands are full

Entry friction is the number-one risk in the brief and the number-one complaint in the category. The market ships an add path — and its own users say it fails. J2 rather than J1 because J2 is where staleness starts.

2

J3 — believing it without going to check

Existential and unopposed. No listed competitor confirms freshness; the etalon is Waze, not a medicine app. Its mechanism is not in the locked v1 scope — the one-tap confirm and the visible age exist only in the research.

3

S1 — not being the one it all depends on

The Keeper's side of the Main job, and the only route by which the Main job — P2's, the reason the product exists — is ever served. Nobody ships the form that works: household sharing with an honest data declaration.

Not core, but binding — E1 and E2

Both score 3 for P1 and both are unaddressed, and neither is a job you build an MVP around: they are conditions the three above must satisfy. E1 — silence as the default — decides whether J3's confirmations get muted. E2 — never cap the number of medicines — decides whether J1's hours turn into the most resented betrayal in the category.

Why J1, J4 and Main are not in the core. J1 is done once and people do get through it; it is the price of entry, and shares J2's function. J4 is addressed by the market — the only distinction is the honesty of the declaration. Main is P2's, its P1 cell is [?], and S1 is its Keeper-side expression.

Candidate functions for removal

Tier 1 — addresses no job in this document

Kit gaps — a curated reference kit against what the household owns. No row asks for it, and it leans toward telling people what they should have, the boundary the brief draws. Cut from v1, or find the job first.

Chronic conditions on member profiles — no v1 flag reads this field; only allergies feed a warning. Special-category GDPR data collected for nothing in v1. Cut until a flag needs it.

The why in the dose log — who and when serve the Main job's third question; why serves nothing, and is a free-text field asked of someone unwell. Cut the field, keep the log.
